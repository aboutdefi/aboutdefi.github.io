Fairshake backs 32 House candidates as crypto bill stalls

Fairshake named 32 House candidates for support, including six set for $1 million each, as the crypto industry looks to revive stalled legislation in the next Congress.

The Blocktape Editors Oct 8, 2026 · 11:04 UTC2 min read 177c24

Fairshake said on Oct. 5 it would support 32 House candidates from both parties ahead of the November midterms, naming six for $1 million each as the crypto industry looks to revive stalled legislation. The Associated Press report said the slate includes 19 Republicans and 13 Democrats, all of whom supported legislation that was a top industry priority.

Which House candidates will get the $1 million allocations?

Fairshake named Democrats Janelle Bynum of Oregon, Steven Horsford of Nevada and Derek Tran of California, and Republicans French Hill of Arkansas, Bryan Steil of Wisconsin and Bill Huizenga of Michigan. Each is set to receive $1 million in support, according to Roll Call’s report.

Fairshake did not disclose how much it plans to spend on the other 26 candidates. The group has described its approach as backing crypto-friendly candidates from both parties; the six announced allocations are split evenly between Democrats and Republicans.

What happened to the industry’s priority bill?

The Senate blocked the CLARITY Act in September, stalling an industry-backed effort to establish a federal framework for digital asset markets. The Associated Press reported that Democrats helped block the bill after seeking limits on President Donald Trump’s crypto investments.

Fairshake announced the House slate as the industry looks to the next Congress to revive legislation that stalled this year. In September, the group also announced plans to spend nearly $30 million opposing Democratic Senate candidate Sherrod Brown in Ohio, where he is challenging Republican Sen. Jon Husted.

Fairshake has not said whether it will make additional House or Senate spending commitments before November. The next step for the 32 House candidates is the midterm election; any effort to revive the stalled legislation would come in the next Congress.
