Senate Passes DeFi Rule Repeal, Leaving Tax Reporting Unsettled

The 70-28 Senate vote blocks an IRS plan to make DeFi front ends report customer sales, but it does not erase taxpayer duty to report crypto gains.

By Crypto Infrastructure Times Editorial Sep 7, 2026 3 min read

Filed under

The Senate passed H.J.Res. 25 by 70-28 on March 26, 2025, and Congress later made it law, nullifying the IRS DeFi broker rule; the result removes an unworkable reporting mandate without removing anyone’s obligation to report taxable crypto gains. The rule was never a new crypto tax. It was an attempt to make parts of the non-custodial stack collect user information and report gross proceeds to the IRS. That distinction matters because the burden would have landed on software and access layers that do not hold customer assets, control settlement, or necessarily know who is behind a wallet.

What did the IRS DeFi broker rule require?

It would have treated some DeFi front-end providers as brokers when they regularly provided services that effectuated digital-asset sales for consideration. That could include interfaces giving users access to automated market makers, order matching, market-making functions, or other trading protocols. Treasury estimated that roughly 650 to 875 DeFi brokers could be affected, with a midpoint of about 765.

The rule focused on reporting gross proceeds through Form 1099-DA, along with collecting taxpayer information and handling backup withholding. It did not calculate a trader’s profit, and it did not create a new obligation to pay tax on a token sale. It created an obligation to build the reporting machinery around the sale.

Why was the DeFi broker rule hard to operate?

It was hard to operate because a permissionless protocol can execute a trade without creating a customer account, taking custody, or learning the trader’s legal identity. A wallet address is not a tax profile. A front end may route one transaction across several pools, while validators merely attest to the resulting state and do not see a complete picture of the user’s basis or beneficial ownership.

The practical mandate would have pushed independent operators toward an identity and compliance layer:

  • Collect names, taxpayer identification numbers, and transaction records from pseudonymous users.
  • Reconcile routed trades and wallet activity into reportable gross proceeds.
  • Store sensitive customer data even when the underlying protocol never needed it.
  • Implement withholding and penalty controls without custody of the asset or control over the user’s wallet.

That is a centralization cost, not a minor paperwork cost. The node can remain online, but the independent interface, RPC service, or small developer team may stop serving U.S. users rather than assume liability for data it cannot reliably obtain. The rule’s estimated broker count also understated the operational question: a single protocol can have many interfaces, relays, libraries, and routing services, each unsure whether it had crossed the broker line.

What does repealing the rule change for DeFi?

Repeal removes the special DeFi broker mandate, but it does not repeal the tax code or eliminate reporting by custodial exchanges and other traditional brokers. Users still owe tax on taxable gains and still need records of purchases, sales, exchanges, and basis. A validator that only performs consensus work is not transformed into a tax intermediary merely because transactions pass through the chain.

The Senate reached the right operational verdict: reporting should follow custody, customer accounts, and control over execution—not every independent operator that publishes code, relays data, or validates blocks. Congress can still write a narrower regime for identifiable intermediaries. Any replacement should draw that line by mechanism, so a company holding customer assets faces reporting duties while a non-custodial node is not forced to become a surveillance endpoint.
